# Helios: 3D Crop Modeling for Environment-Aware Breeding

## What Is Helios?

Helios is an open-source, three-dimensional plant and canopy model developed at the University of California, Davis. It builds a virtual plant organ by organ, leaf by leaf, and simulates the biological and physical processes that take place inside and around each organ: how leaves expand and age, how sunlight is captured and turned into sugars through photosynthesis, how water moves through the plant and is lost through the stomata, and how carbon is partitioned among leaves, stems, roots, and grain. Because every process is grounded in physics and plant physiology, Helios is a mechanistic, whole-plant model. It contains the components a plant needs to grow and a canopy needs to develop. The result is a "digital twin" of a crop that grows in a realistic environment the way a real plant would. Researchers can change one factor at a time, such as a trait, a weather pattern, or a management practice, and see how the plant responds.

## The GEMINI Project

GEMINI is an international research project that brings together plant breeding, high-throughput phenotyping, and crop modeling to support smallholder farmers in Sub-Saharan Africa. It focuses on two staple crops of the region, sorghum and cowpea. Breeding sits at the center of the project. Every season, breeders must decide which lines to cross, which to advance, and where a new variety should be released. Those decisions depend on how a variety will perform across the wide range of soils, climates, and farming practices in the region, known as genotype × environment × management (G×E×M) interactions. Field trials can only sample a small share of these conditions, and the data they produce are often sparse and noisy. GEMINI aims to fill this gap by combining field measurements with predictive modeling, so breeders can estimate how a genotype will perform in places and seasons where it has not yet been tested. Helios is the modeling engine behind this effort.

## Inside Helios

Helios is organized as a set of connected modules, or plug-ins, each of which represents one part of the plant–environment system. A plant architecture module grows each plant through its developmental stages and captures traits such as plant height, leaf angle, branching pattern, and leaf area, with ready-made models for crops including sorghum and cowpea. A radiation module uses GPU-accelerated ray tracing to follow sunlight as it is absorbed, reflected, and scattered through the canopy, so the light reaching every individual leaf is known. This light then drives the physiology modules, which simulate photosynthesis, stomatal conductance, leaf energy balance and temperature, and water transport through the plant's hydraulic system, while carbon and nitrogen budgets track how resources are supplied and used as the plant grows. Helios can also act as a virtual sensor, producing synthetic camera images, LiDAR scans, and labeled imagery of the simulated plants that can be compared directly with images collected in the field. Built-in parameter-optimization tools then adjust the model until the virtual plant matches the real one. Helios is freely available, with a C++ core, a Python interface (PyHelios), and a graphical user interface.

## Helios in GEMINI Phase I

In GEMINI Phase I, the team showed that Helios could serve as the bridge between field phenotyping and prediction for sorghum and cowpea. Breeding plots at partner field stations were imaged at high resolution by ground-based robotic platforms and low-altitude drones. These images, combined with manual field measurements, were used to estimate genotype-specific parameters in Helios that describe how each variety builds its canopy and responds to its environment. Because Helios can render what a camera would see, the model and field images could be compared directly. This simulation-to-reality link made it possible to extract physiological information from imagery at scale. Helios was also extended for the GEMINI crops, including new architectural models and leaf optical properties for cowpea and common bean measured in GEMINI field experiments. Together, these advances established a working proof of concept: a pipeline that goes from field sensing, to a parameterized virtual crop, to performance predictions across environments.

## Lessons From Phase I

Phase I also made clear where the approach needed to grow. Predictions were strong when a genotype or an environment had already been observed, but they remained hardest in the case breeders care about most: predicting how a *new* genotype will perform in a *new* environment. A second limitation concerned yield. Traits tied to yield formation, such as how assimilates are allocated to grain during grain fill, cannot be reliably estimated from imagery alone. The model's representation of abiotic stresses such as drought and heat was also less developed than its treatment of photosynthesis, stomatal behavior, and plant architecture. Since yield and yield stability under stress are what ultimately determine a variety's value to farmers, closing this gap is essential.

## GEMINI Phase II: From Proof of Concept to a Breeder-Ready Toolkit

GEMINI Phase II builds directly on these lessons. Its goal is to turn the Phase I proof of concept into an operational G×E×M prediction toolkit that breeding programs can use in their routine work on hybrid sorghum and cowpea. Phase II combines high-throughput phenotyping at on-station sites, a pipeline that uses detailed soil and weather data with crop modeling to define the Target Population of Environments (TPE) each variety must serve, and the delivery of these tools through the Helios user interface and the GEMINI App. These tools are designed for partner programs including CIMMYT-Kenya, IITA-Kano, Makerere University, ISRA/CERAAS-Senegal, and the ADCIN and West African cowpea regional networks. Within this effort, Helios will advance in two key directions.

**Predicting performance in untested environments.** Phase II will extend Helios so that parameters estimated for a genotype at a few intensively measured field stations (Kiboko in Kenya, IITA-Kano in Nigeria, and supporting sites in California) can be used to predict its performance anywhere across Sub-Saharan Africa. To do this, the model will draw on gridded satellite and reanalysis datasets for weather and soil, including ERA5-Land, MERRA-2, IMERG, and iSDAsoil. These will be checked against ground measurements from partner programs. Climate projections will be added to give breeders a roughly ten-year forward-looking view of how their target environments may shift. Each prediction will carry a measure of uncertainty, and predictions will be tested against regional trial networks, with particular focus on the new-genotype, new-environment case where Phase I fell short.

**Modeling yield and stress tolerance.** Phase II will also develop a carbohydrate-dynamics module tailored to sorghum and cowpea. It will track how sugars produced by photosynthesis are supplied, allocated, and later remobilized between source organs (leaves and stems) and sink organs (pods, panicles, and grain) through vegetative growth, flowering, and grain fill. This module will link the canopy processes Helios already simulates to the outcomes breeders value most: grain yield, seed weight, fodder yield in dual-purpose cowpea, and yield stability under stress. It will also represent how drought, heat around flowering, and nutrient limitation disrupt this carbon balance. These stresses are priorities in partner breeding programs. To estimate yield traits that are hard to measure from images, the team will combine harvest measurements with genomic information.

Together, these advances will give breeders a mechanistic, environment-aware view of how their varieties are likely to perform, both where they are grown today and where they may be grown in the future. All results, software, and tools will be shared openly through publications and the public Helios and GEMINI App repositories.
